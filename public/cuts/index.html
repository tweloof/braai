The buying guide

Befriend a butcher.
It outperforms most investments.

A braai is only ever as good as what you put on the grid. Here's what to ask for — animal by animal — and why the cheapest tray in the supermarket fridge is usually the most expensive mistake of the weekend.

First, the golden rules

Buy thick — a steak under 3 cm cooks to grey before it ever sears properly. Buy fat — fat is where the flavour lives, and open coals were made for rendering it. And buy from someone who can tell you where the meat comes from and how long it's been hung; a butcher who ages beef properly is worth a detour across town.

Beef

For open coals you want tender cuts with fat through or around them: rump (the braai favourite — flavour first, with a proper fat cap), sirloin, ribeye and the mighty T-bone, which gives you fillet and sirloin either side of a bone that keeps everything juicy. A prime rib on the bone, cut thick, is the special-occasion move. Brisket, chuck and shin are wonderful animals' cuts but they want hours of moist heat — that's potjie territory, not grid territory.

Lamb

The tjop is the aristocrat of the braai. Loin tjops for tenderness, rib tjops for that crisp fat edge that never makes it to the plate, chump tjops for a bigger, meatier chew. A deboned, butterflied leg is the finest way to feed eight people who think they don't like lamb. And lamb ribbetjies, braaied slowly until the fat renders crisp, are the reason the braaier eats before everyone else — quality control, you understand.

Pork

The most underrated meat on the South African braai, and the best value on the block. The star is the pork neck steak — beautifully marbled, almost impossible to dry out, and happier over coals than almost any beef cut at twice the price. Spare ribs want the slow-then-sticky treatment (recipe here), belly slices crisp up gloriously over a medium fire, and a smoked kassler chop needs only warming and light charring — it's already seasoned better than most of us season anything. Pork wants a medium fire and should be cooked through but never cremated: juicy, not leathery.

Chicken

The braai's great test of patience. A whole spatchcocked (flattie) chicken over medium coals, turned often and basted late, beats any takeaway rotisserie. Thighs forgive; breasts hold grudges — choose accordingly. Wings char beautifully and vanish first, and sosaties (recipe here) solve the how-do-I-feed-twelve problem for the price of a tray of thigh fillets. Chicken and fierce flames end badly: keep it over the medium zone and let the skin render slowly.

Boerewors — the real thing

By law, anything sold in South Africa as boerewors must be at least 90% meat — the rest is spice, vinegar and tradition, with coriander doing the heavy lifting. The cheaper coils labelled "braaiwors" or "grillers" don't have to meet that bar, and it shows. Look for a coarse cut, visible fat, a natural casing, and a butcher who guards the recipe like a family secret — because it usually is one. Then treat it with respect: whole on the grid, turned seldom, never pierced.
