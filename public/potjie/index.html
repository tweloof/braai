The potjiekos guide · a braai.co.za tradition since 2009

The pot that must
not be stirred.

Potjiekos — "small-pot food" — is the braai's patient cousin: a three-legged cast-iron pot, a handful of coals, a strict law of layers, and hours of doing gloriously little.

Potjiekos is a slow-cooked stew-that-is-not-a-stew, made in a round-bellied, three-legged cast-iron pot (the potjie) over a small bed of coals. Meat is browned in the bottom, vegetables are layered on top in strict order, a modest amount of liquid goes in, the lid goes on — and then, for the next three hours, the only acceptable intervention is adding the occasional coal and defending the pot from people holding spoons. The flavours layer instead of blending, which is the whole point, and the difference between a potjie and a stew is the difference between a choir and a shout.

The pot

Potjies are numbered, not measured — a tradition nobody questions. A no. 2 feeds four comfortably, a no. 3 (the people's choice) feeds six to eight, and anything bigger is for weddings and rugby finals. New pots need seasoning: scrub out the factory coating, rub with oil, and bake it black over a fire once or twice. A well-loved potjie is never scrubbed with soap again — wipe it, oil it, and let the years do the seasoning. Inheriting one is better than inheriting money.

The law of layers

Everything about a potjie follows from one principle: what needs longest cooks lowest. Brown the meat first in the bottom of the pot, in the fat, in batches. Soften your onions in the same fat — they carry the flavour up. Then layer: hard vegetables (carrots, potatoes) before soft ones (mushrooms, beans), starch where the recipe wants it, and just enough liquid to steam the tower without drowning it — a potjie is not a soup, and the lid returns most of what rises. Then the lid goes on and the law takes over.

The no-stirring rule

You do not stir a potjie. Not to check, not to help, not because you were nervous. Stirring collapses the layers, turns dinner into ordinary stew, and — more seriously — announces to everyone present that you panicked. The one sanctioned exception: a gentle fold in the final minutes when a recipe finishes with cream or seasoning. Otherwise the lid stays on, the steam does the work, and you occupy your hands with a dop and your reputation with patience.

The coals

A potjie wants far fewer coals than a braai — a small steady bed underneath and a few fresh ones added every half hour from a feeder fire burning alongside. The pot should murmur, never gallop: a lazy simmer you can hear when you put your ear near the lid (an accepted potjie ritual). Too hot and the bottom catches while the top steams; if you smell catching, take coals away — you can always add, you can never un-burn. Wood, timing and the hand test all live in the Wood & Coals guide.

Potjie or braai? Both. The classic move is a potjie for the main event with a braaibroodjie intermission — the pot forgives you for opening the grid, as long as you don't touch the lid.

The three classics

Chicken, bacon & mushroom potjie

No. 3 potjie · gentle coals Serves 6 ±2½ hours

The creamy crowd-pleaser — a favourite on this site since the 2009 blog days, rebuilt properly. Bacon starts it, cream finishes it, and everything in between obeys the law of layers.

Ingredients

  • 250 g streaky bacon, chopped
  • 1.5 kg chicken thighs & drumsticks
  • 2 onions, sliced
  • 2 cloves garlic, crushed
  • 500 g button mushrooms, halved
  • 3 carrots, thickly sliced
  • 500 g baby potatoes
  • 250 ml chicken stock
  • 250 ml cream
  • 2 tsp fresh thyme, salt & black pepper

Method

  1. Render the bacon in the potjie over good coals until it gives up its fat. Brown the chicken in that fat, skin down first.
  2. Tuck the onions and garlic in around the chicken and let them soften.
  3. Layer: mushrooms on the chicken, carrots on the mushrooms, potatoes on top. Pour the stock over. Lid on.
  4. Simmer over a few steady coals for 1½–2 hours. Do not stir. Do not peek more than twice. You will be judged.
  5. Fold in the cream and thyme for the last 15 minutes — the one legal stir — season, and serve with rice or pap.

Curried pork potjie

No. 3 potjie · gentle coals Serves 6 ±3 hours

Another 2009 original reborn. Pork neck — the most underrated cut on the block — in a mild curry warmed with apricot, the great South African pairing.

Ingredients

  • 1.5 kg pork neck, cubed
  • 2 tbsp oil
  • 2 onions, sliced
  • 2 tbsp mild curry powder + 1 tsp turmeric
  • 2 cloves garlic + 1 tbsp grated ginger
  • 3 carrots, thickly sliced
  • 500 g potatoes, quartered
  • 1 tin (410 g) chopped tomatoes
  • 2 tbsp smooth apricot jam
  • 250 ml stock, 2 bay leaves, salt & pepper

Method

  1. Brown the pork in oil in the potjie — in batches, so it browns rather than boils.
  2. Add the onions, curry powder, turmeric, garlic and ginger and fry until your neighbours start asking questions.
  3. Layer the carrots, then the potatoes, on top of the meat. Mix the tomatoes, jam and stock and pour over. Bay leaves in, lid on.
  4. Simmer over a few steady coals for 2–2½ hours without stirring, until the pork surrenders completely.
  5. Season, and serve over pap or rice with something cold nearby.

Beef & red wine potjie

No. 3 potjie · gentle coals Serves 8 ±4 hours

The canon. Shin and chuck — the cuts the grid can't tame — browned hard, layered high, and left alone until the wine, the marrow and the hours have done what no rushing ever could.

Ingredients

  • 1.5 kg beef shin & chuck, cubed
  • 3 tbsp flour, seasoned
  • 3 tbsp oil
  • 2 onions, sliced + 3 cloves garlic
  • 250 ml dry red wine
  • 250 ml beef stock
  • 4 carrots, thickly sliced
  • 250 g button mushrooms
  • 500 g baby potatoes
  • 200 g green beans
  • 2 sprigs rosemary, 2 bay leaves, salt & pepper

Method

  1. Toss the beef in seasoned flour. Brown it hard in oil, in batches — the crust is the flavour bank.
  2. Soften the onions and garlic in the same pot, then deglaze with the wine and let it bubble for a minute. Add the stock and herbs.
  3. Layer: carrots, then mushrooms, then potatoes, green beans on top. Lid on.
  4. Simmer over a few steady coals for 3–3½ hours. The pot murmurs; you kuier. That is the entire division of labour.
  5. Season and serve from the pot, at the fire, with roosterkoek for the gravy.
